## The one idea, this round

**Every defect fixed today was a matching failure, not a maths failure.** The
prices were right and sitting in `data/costs.csv`. Nothing could reach them,
because two sides of a join disagreed about a string.

- `"mL"` is not `"ml"`, so the entire Harry Gatos scrape was excluded from the
  cost book by capitalisation.
- `"[Bottle]"` has no size in it, so a spirit priced per bottle could not be
  divided into a 30 ml pour.
- `"Vermouth Blend"` is not `"Stow Vermouth Blend"`, so four cocktails poured
  the house blend for free.
- `products_weekly` collapses `- Pint` and `- Schooner` into one drink; the
  audit did not, so every tap beer read as having no sales at all.

None of them threw. Each one resolved to `$0.00`, which reads as 100% gross
profit — the flattering direction, which is the dangerous one.

### 1. The Harry Gatos scrape could never reach the cost book

Our invoice-fed price is used only when its unit matches how the recipe uses the
line, and that check was a raw string compare. Stowaway's scrape writes `ml`;
Harry Gatos' writes `mL`, `L`, `kg` and `Units`. 299 lines were excluded on
capitalisation alone, 162 of them with a real cost already in `data/costs.csv`.

`normalise_line_units()` now puts every line in base units before anything reads
it. 0.27 L is 270 ml and 2.84 kg is 2840 g — the quantity moves with the label,
never without. An unrecognised label is left visible rather than guessed.

### 2. …and that exposed 23 bulk-label typos underneath it

Shiitake Tare draws "1600 L" of soy. Gochujang Honey Soy is 11,000 litres. The
cook typed the millilitre figure and left the product's bulk label beside it —
the same defect `data/recipe_line_unit_fixes.yaml` was opened for with Peking
Sauce, but Peking Sauce could be proved from its declared yield and none of
these 13 Harry Gatos batches has one.

The proof that does run is in the book itself: **Harry Gatos holds its chilli
sauce typed both ways.** "HG's Soy Chilli Sauce" reads 2.5 L soy + 0.25 kg
chilli; "HG Soy Chilli Sauce" reads 2500 L + 250 kg. Same sauce, exactly 1000x
apart. After the fix the two twins agree to 0.8%, and the residual is real
(Garlic Peeled vs Garlic Minced, caster vs white sugar).

The rule is deliberately narrow: only `L`/`kg` labels, never `ml`/`g` — reading
a base unit as bulk is what took Rosemary Salted Fries from $1.86 to $0.0019 —
and only above 25 L/kg, which clears the largest real line in the book (Achiote
Chicken's 15 kg) with a wide margin. Corpse Reviver No. 2 sits under that bound
at "20 L" of triple sec, so it is named in the yaml with its own proof: the
drink's other three lines are 20 mL each.

Every application is printed on every run.

### 3. A spirit sold by the millilitre is now costed by the millilitre

The bottle SKU that holds the price is named "Four Pillars Olive Leaf [Bottle]",
and there is no size in the word "Bottle". So the seeder priced a whole bottle
as one countable "can"; a 30 ml pour asked for 30 cans of gin, the unit clash
was refused, and three spirits selling at $14.50–$19.00 costed $0.00.

The size was in the Back Office export all along, in `Unit` and `DefaultSize`.
Those two columns contradict each other across the catalogue — Sriracha reads
`l` / 730 while its name says 730 mL — so the NAME still wins wherever it states
a pack, and the declared pack is a fallback with both readings bounded.

Also: having a cost row is not the same as being costed. A per-"each" price
cannot be multiplied by a 30 ml pour, so a whole-pack price may now be re-seeded
per ml. A per-ml one may not — that is an invoice or a better seed.

### 4. Zero is not a price

10 products reached the P&L at exactly $0.00. None is free; each is a real pour
whose product carries no cost anywhere. Publishing that 0 does not report "we
don't know" — it reports a $40 glass of rosé at 100% GP, silently. The book no
longer offers a $0 as a cost; Lightspeed's figure is wrong too, but it is
visibly sourced (`cost_source='lightspeed'`) and the audit is already shouting.

### 6. Two more name-matching failures, found by following the coverage list

**Four Harry Gatos cocktails poured the house vermouth for free.**
`recipe_venue_mirrors.yaml` already records that "Vermouth Blend" and "Stow
Vermouth Blend" are the same liquid, and already mirrors Americano,
Boulevardier, Manhattan - Perfect and Sbagliato Negroni to Stowaway. But a
mirror is a RECIPE-level statement and these four reach the blend through an
INGREDIENT line, so the decision was made and never applied.

**A wine called "Red Blend" was filed as a batch.** The prep classifier reads
the recipe name for `blend|batch|mix|prep|marination` and bracket sizes, and a
prep is excluded from the P&L's cost book. "Sigurd GSM Red Blend" matched on
"Blend"; "Yuzushu [60ml]" and "Kunizakari Umeshu [60ml]" matched on their serve
size. $2,417 of wine costed off Lightspeed instead of our own $4.62 a glass.

Back Office settles it without a judgement call: all 64 real preps carry a blank
ReportingGroup and a $0 price, so a product filed under a menu category at a
menu price is something a customer orders whatever its name says. The lookup is
on the EXACT product name — `norm()` strips the bracket, so "Stow Vermouth Blend
[Bottle]" and "[30ml]" collapse to one key, and they are precisely the pair that
must not.

## Bugs found while verifying, not by looking for them

**The ingredient picker showed the OLDEST price in its 90-day window.**
`cogs_list.csv` is sorted oldest-first and `build_ingredients` kept the first row
it met. 257 of 477 ingredients sat on a superseded row; 98 had since changed
price. Sriracha read $7.10 against a current $4.70. A tin of black beans read
$52.20 against $8.70 — a 6x overstatement in the UI a chef builds recipes in.

**CI was lying, in three separate ways.**

1. A pytest-shaped test written under `scripts/` is never run — not by `pytest`
   (`testpaths` is pinned to `modules/`) and not by CI, which names the
   script-shaped ones one at a time. `scripts/test_our_costs_book.py` sat like
   that: 6 tests on the P&L's use of the cost book, green whenever pointed at,
   never once executed. `pyproject.toml` now says where a new test belongs.
2. The pipeline seam test read `costs.csv`'s OLDEST row while every consumer
   reads the latest, so it failed on products whose unit legitimately improved.
   It now compares the **rate in base units** — strictly stronger, since a real
   12x disagreement would fail where a unit-string compare passed.
3. `data/ingredients.json` is deliberately uncommitted (a 90-day window off
   `date.today()`), and `audit_book` read it blind. Green on every developer
   tree, red on CI's clean checkout, twenty seconds in.

The lesson worth keeping: **verify against a fresh clone with no generated
files.** Two of the four would have been caught immediately.

## A rule I built and threw away — do not rebuild it

**"The dish names an ingredient its recipe does not contain."** It looked like
the general form of "Hawaiian had no ham", and it does catch the real ones:
Seitan Katsu Curry has no seitan, Lemon Lime & Bitters has neither lemon nor
lime, Stow Soda has no soda.

It also produced 373 findings at roughly 5% precision. Cocktails are named after
nothing you can buy (Mojito, Daiquiri, Bombardino) and pizzas are named after
people (Sanchez, Paddock, New Yorker). Restricting the missing word to one that
names a stocked product cut it to 236 and barely moved the precision — every
apparent hit I checked was a synonym: Rosemary Salted Fries has "Farm Frites
Crispy Coated", Sensei Calamari has "Pineapple Cut Squid", Wok Green Beans has
"Bean Green [kg]".

A rule at that precision makes the audit worse, not better, because it teaches
whoever reads it to skim. Making it work needs a maintained ingredient-word
list, which is exactly the kind of thing that rots. Left unbuilt on purpose.

What *is* worth knowing from the exercise: several dishes carry no oil, no
seasoning and no garnish at all — Rosemary Salted Fries is one line of frozen
chips, Popcorn Cauliflower has no batter. Those are real gaps, but each needs a
quantity from the kitchen, not a rule.

## Known limitation, stated not hidden

The costed book still carries no effective date, so `_load_book_costs` answers
"what does this cost now", not "what did it cost in June". Right for the daily
6am pull, approximate for a historical backfill. It never overrides a dated
source and only ever displaces Lightspeed's stale figure. **Making the book
effective-dated remains the clean follow-up** and would close the last as-of gap.
